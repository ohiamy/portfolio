    Gudetama Tamagotchi: A Stress-Relieving Desktop Companion

    A browser and desktop app prototype built entirely with Figma Make — featuring original illustrations I drew myself. Born from a stressful quarter at work and a curiosity about what Figma Make could actually do.

    Context

    Figma Make access + a stressful quarter = Gudetama

    When we got access to Figma Make at work, I knew I wanted to explore it — but I didn't want my first experiment to be something high-stakes. I wanted to play. And honestly, work had been exhausting, so the project had a personal brief from the start: build something that makes me feel better just by looking at it.

    Gudetama — Sanrio's notoriously lazy egg yolk who can't be bothered to face the day — felt like the perfect muse. A tiny desktop companion that asks almost nothing of you and just vibes. It's basically stress relief with a face.

    Process

    Drawing first, building second

    I started in Figma — not prototyping, just drawing. I created all the illustrations and image assets from scratch: Gudetama in various lazy states, expressions for different interactions, background elements. Getting the look right before touching Figma Make meant I had a visual language to build toward, rather than retrofitting assets into whatever the prototype happened to become.

    For the Figma Make side, I worked with Databricks AI to help craft and refine my prompts. AI-assisted prompting was new territory for me, and having a sounding board to pressure-test my descriptions helped me communicate intent more precisely than I would have on my own.

    From there it was genuinely iterative — I'd add a feature, realize it needed a new asset, go back to Figma to draw it, then bring it back in. The loop felt natural, almost like how I'd develop a design system incrementally, except the deliverable was a tiny lazy egg who sighs when you poke him.

    Image placeholder: Custom Gudetama illustration assets
    Reflection

    What I was actually testing

    This was a tool exploration as much as a design project. I wanted to understand Figma Make's ceiling — what kind of interactivity could I get from prompts alone, and where did I need to be more directive? The answer turned out to be: it handles a surprising amount if your prompts are specific, and it falls apart quickly if they're vague. Pairing it with Databricks AI to sharpen my language before sending prompts made a real difference.

    There's also something worth noting about the illustration work. I don't often get to do purely expressive visual design in my day-to-day — most of my work is systems and flows and research. Drawing Gudetama being deeply unbothered was a nice reminder that I enjoy it, and that loosening up visually makes me a better designer overall.

    Outcomes

    Did it work?

    As a stress reliever: yes. As a Figma Make experiment: also yes. The prototype ended up functional enough to actually use — a little interactive desktop companion that does exactly what it says on the tin.

    The reception from people who saw it was warm. "Cute" was the word I heard most, which was exactly the goal. Sometimes the right KPI is just: does this make someone smile?
